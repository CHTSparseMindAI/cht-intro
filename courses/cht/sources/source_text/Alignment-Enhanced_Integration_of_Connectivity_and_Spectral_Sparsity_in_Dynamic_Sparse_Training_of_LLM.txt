011                                              A BSTRACT
012
013            With the rapid development of large language models (LLMs), identifying effi-
014
               cient strategies for training such large-scale systems has become increasingly crit-
               ical. Although LLMs have achieved remarkable success across diverse applica-
015
               tions, the necessity of maintaining full dense matrices during pre-training has been
016
               questioned, giving rise to parameter-efficient sparse pre-training methods which
017            retains parameter-efficiency in both training and inference. These methods can be
018            further divided into connectivity sparse training and spectral sparse training, with
019            dynamic connectivity sparse training and low-rank factorization emerging as rep-
020            resentative approaches for the two branches. However, a unified framework that
021            effectively combines the strengths of both has yet to be established. In this work,
022            we observe that the cancellation effect between the sparse and low-rank branches
023            may limit the expressivity of the model, manifesting as output conflicts when the
024            two components are combined. To address this issue, we propose a novel scheme
025
               that integrates dynamic sparse training with low-rank training, introducing a sim-
               ple yet effective alignment loss to mitigate the disagreement between the two
026
               branches and promote better collaboration. We validate this scheme by combin-
027
               ing a representative dynamic sparse training method, CHTs, with low-rank train-
028            ing, resulting in a new parameter-efficient training approach termed CHTsL. The
029            method is evaluated on LLaMA60M and LLaMA130M using the OpenWebText
030            and C4 datasets, where only 10%, 20%, and 30% of the parameters are preserved
031            compared to dense training. Experimental results demonstrate that our proposed
032            scheme effectively alleviates the cancellation effect and improves training stabil-
033            ity and performance compared to the naive combination of sparse and low-rank
034            components. Also, the new scheme enables CHTsL to consistently outperform
035            other parameter-efficient sparse training methods under the same parameter bud-
036
               get, achieving performance most close to dense training.

038   1   I NTRODUCION
039
      Large language models (LLMs) have attracted tremendous attention due to their superior perfor-
040
      mance across a wide range of tasks. Despite their impressive capabilities, training LLMs from
041
      scratch remains extremely memory-intensive and computation-intensive (Samsi et al., 2023), mak-
042   ing it challenging to scale such models under reasonable resource constraints. This has motivated
043   extensive research on efficient methods that reduce computational and memory costs while retaining
044   competitive performance. One of the most direct strategies is to reduce the number of parameters.
045   Early studies on pruning and low-rank fine-tuning (Hu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023; Renduchin-
046   tala et al., 2023; Sheng et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Kopiczko et al., 2023; Dettmers et al., 2023)
047   have shown that even after removing or compressing a large fraction of parameters, models can still
048   preserve much of their original representational capacity. These findings suggest that parameter-
049   efficient model manipulation is feasible, and they naturally motivate the extension from pruning or
050
      finetuning to sparse pretraining, where models are trained from scratch under constrained parameter
      budgets while maintaining competitive performance compared with dense training.
051
052   We divides current approaches to sparse pre-training can be broadly into two branches: connec-
053   tivity sparse training and spectral sparse training, which refers to those methods utilizing low-rank
      factorization during pretraining.

054
      The former branch focuses on enforcing sparsity in the connectivity of weight matrices, with dy-
055   namic connectivity sparse training emerging as a representative technique(Mocanu et al., 2018;
056   Jayakumar et al., 2020; Evci et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024; 2025). Dynamic
057   connectivity sparse training maintains a sparse connectivity pattern throughout pre-training, dynam-
058   ically changing the sparse connectivity and updating active weights to approximate the capacity
059   of dense models. Recent works have shown that on multiple tasks, dynamic sparse training can
060   approach or even surpass the performance of dense models with as little as 10% of the trainable
061   parameters, marking a significant step forward in efficient training(Zhang et al., 2024).
062   The second branch, spectral sparse training (Zhao et al., 2024a), is typically instantiated through
063   low-rank factorization. Since the low-rank factors are updated during training, the spectral rep-
064   resentation they induce also evolves accordingly, which makes spectral sparse training inherently
065   dynamic. Initially proposed in the context of LLM fine-tuning (Hu et al., 2022), low-rank methods
066   decompose weight matrices into low-dimensional components, training only the low-rank repre-
067   sentations while freezing the full-rank backbone. These approaches drastically reduce the number
068   of trainable parameters and leverage the pre-trained dense model’s representational power. More
069
      recent attempts have extended low-rank factorization to the pre-training stage (Lialin et al., 2023;
      Zhao et al., 2024b; Xia et al., 2024; Meng et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024a). However, these methods
070
      still require the use of full dense matrices during the forward pass, rather than maintaining the spec-
071
      tral sparse structure consistently from training to inference. Overcoming this limitation, successors
072   like CoLA (Liu et al., 2025) preserve the low-rank structure throughout both training and inference,
073   further validating the feasibility of spectral sparse training.
074
075
      While previous attempt SLTrain (Han et al., 2024) explored combining sparse and low-rank com-
      ponents, the design remains limited in two key aspects. First, the sparse branch in SLTrain is static,
076
      serving only as a supplementary term to spectral sparse training rather than leveraging the full poten-
077
      tial of dynamic connectivity sparse methods. Second, SLTrain simply performs a pure summation
078   of sparse and low-rank outputs, without any mechanism to promote effective interaction.
079
080   In this work, we take a step in this direction. We observe that naive integration of sparse and low-
081
      rank branches often suffers from a cancellation effect, where the two components produce conflict-
      ing representations that weaken expressivity and hinder convergence. To address this challenge, we
082
      propose a new scheme that integrates dynamic connectivity sparse training with low-rank training
083
      under the guidance of alignment loss, which aligns the two branches and promotes cooperative learn-
084   ing. Specifically, we instantiate our framework by combining the advanced dynamic sparse training
085   method CHTs (Zhang et al., 2025) with low-rank factorization, resulting in a new parameter-efficient
086   pre-training approach, CHTsL. Extensive experiments on LLaMA-60M and LLaMA-130M (Tou-
087   vron et al., 2023a;b) with OpenWebText and C4 show that CHTsL consistently outperforms state-
088   of-the-art parameter-efficient sparse training baselines under the same parameter budget. Notably,
089   with only 10%, 20%, or 30% of parameters preserved relative to dense training, CHTsL achieves
090   performance closest to dense models, which would benefit by retaining efficiency in training, infer-
091   ence, and storage.

      make the first attempt to genuinely integrate connectivity sparse and spectral sparse in dynamic

105
      Instantiation with CHTsL and empirical superiority. We instantiate the framework by combining

112   2     R ELATED W ORK
113
114   The rapid growth of large language models (LLMs) has stimulated extensive research into improving
115   efficiency in pre-training. Among various directions, parameter-efficient approaches have emerged
116
      as particularly promising, aiming at training models with limited number of parameters without
      significantly sacrificing performance. Broadly, parameter-efficient methods in the context of pre-
117
      training can be divided into two branches: connectivity sparse training, which reduces parameters
118
      by enforcing sparse connectivity patterns, and spectral sparse training, which constrains weight
119   matrices into low-rank subspaces. Dynamic connectivity sparse training and low-rank factorization
120   are the representative approaches for these two paradigms.
121
122
      2.1   DYNAMIC C ONNECTIVITY S PARSE T RAINING
123
124   Connectivity sparsity originates from the classical line of pruning (LeCun et al., 1989; Han et al.,
125   2015; Molchanov et al., 2016), where removing parameters from dense models was shown to pre-
126   serve much of the model’s performance. Inspired by this, researchers began to explore whether spar-
127   sity could be maintained throughout training, rather than applied only as a post-hoc compression.
128   Among these efforts, methods that promote sparse training through dynamic adjustment of connec-
129
      tivity have gained increasing attention, as they often outperform static sparse training approaches
      that prune connections solely at initialization (Prabhu et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2018; Dao et al., 2022;
130
      Stewart et al., 2023). The pioneering work Sparse Evolutionary Training (SET) (Mocanu et al.,
131
      2018) removes links while introducing random rewiring of sparse connections during training to
132   maintain model plasticity. RigL (Evci et al., 2020) further dynamically regrows connections based
133   on gradient for more effective exploration, though it requires computing gradients of the full weight
134   matrix during the backward pass. MEST (Yuan et al., 2021) improves upon this by leveraging both
135   weight and gradient information. CHT (Zhang et al., 2024) and its successor CHTs (Zhang et al.,
136   2025) enhance dynamic sparse training using the Cannistracci-Hebbian theory (Muscoloni et al.,
137   2022) from network science, inspired by brain connectomes, achieving state-of-the-art performance
138   on multiple tasks. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that dynamic sparse training can attain
139   competitive or even superior performance compared to dense training, while using only 10% or
140
      fewer of the parameters (Zhang et al., 2025).
141
142   2.2   L OW- RANK FOR S PECTRAL S PARSE T RAINING
143
      Complementary to connectivity sparsity, spectral sparse training leverages low-rank factorization
144
      to reduce the dimensionality of weight matrices. This idea was first popularized in the fine-tuning
145   setting, where LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) adapts pretrained models by learning only low-rank updates
146   rather than full weight matrices. Subsequent works (Hu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023; Renduch-
147   intala et al., 2023; Sheng et al., 2023; Kopiczko et al., 2023; Dettmers et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024)
148   further demonstrate the effectiveness of low-rank fine-tuning and inspire the exploration of training
149   from scratch with low-rank factorization. ReLoRA (Lialin et al., 2023) introduces reparameteriza-
150   tion to improve training efficiency and stability, while GaLore (Zhao et al., 2024b) reduces memory
151   usage by applying low-rank projections in the gradient space during training. However, a common
152   limitation of these approaches is that the full dense weight matrix is still required during the for-
153
      ward pass, providing parameter efficiency only during training but not during inference. In contrast,
      CoLA (Liu et al., 2025) explicitly maintains the low-rank representation throughout both training
154
      and inference, enabling reduced storage and runtime costs. As a side note, while we previously
155
      discussed the relevance of pruning mainly in the context of connectivity-based sparse training, in
156   contrast to spectral low-rank training, structured pruning can also be viewed as implicitly inducing
157   a low-rank structure in the resulting model. This is because structured-pruned models remove entire
158   channels or filters, which correspond to removing rows or columns in the unfolded weight matrices,
159   thereby potentially reducing their effective rank. Representative structured-pruning-aware works in-
160   clude channel pruning via LASSO (He et al., 2017), network slimming (Liu et al., 2017), and more
161   recently Only-Train-Once (OTO) (Chen et al., 2021), which explicitly consider structural constraints
      during training to improve efficiency for subsequent pruning. In this study, we adopt CoLA (Liu


                                                         3
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




162
      et al., 2025) as the baseline under the same restriction of parameter efficiency in both forward and
163   backward passes.
164
165   2.3   H YBRID ATTEMPT
166
167   Beyond individual paradigms, researchers have also begun to explore combining connectivity and
168   spectral sparsity. SLTrain (Han et al., 2024) represents one of the earliest attempts in this direction.
169   It augments low-rank factorization with a sparse branch, but its design exhibits several limitations.
170
      Specifically, the sparse component is static rather than dynamic, serving merely as a supplemen-
      tary term to spectral sparsity instead of leveraging genuine connectivity sparse training. Moreover,
171
      SLTrain integrates the two branches via a simple summation, without introducing any collaborative
172
      mechanism to exploit their potential synergy. As a result, while SLTrain marks an important step
173   toward hybrid parameter-efficient pre-training, it remains an immature solution, leaving room for
174   more principled approaches.

      3     A LIGNMENT- ENHANCED I NTEGRATION OF C ONNECTIVITY S PARSE AND
178         S PECTRAL SPARSE
179
      In this section, we present a unified approach for combining dynamic sparse training (connectivity
180
      sparse) with low-rank factorization (spectral sparse) under extreme sparsity. While each method
181   alone can improve parameter-efficiency and memory-efficiency, their naive combination often leads
182   to conflicting outputs that limit the model’s effective capacity. We address this challenge with three
183   key steps: (i) identifying and quantifying the cancellation effect, (ii) introducing a training frame-
184   work that stabilizes low-rank outputs and encourages cooperation between branches, and (iii) in-
185   stantiating a method, CHTsL, that integrates connectivity sparse and spectral sparse for dynamic
186   sparse training based on this framework.
187
188   3.1   C ANCELLATION E FFECT AND OCR M ETRIC
189

190
      their outputs sometimes point in opposite directions. This cancellation effect means that some
191   portion of the signal from one branch can be neutralized by the other, wasting representational
192   power. In other words, even if each branch individually carries meaningful information, their naive
193   sum may not fully reflect that information, effectively underutilizing the model’s capacity.
194

483   6      C ONCLUSION
484
485   In this work, we present a novel framework for parameter-efficient pre-training by systematically
      integrating connectivity sparse training with spectral sparse in dynamic sparse training. We identify


                                                                      9
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




486
      the cancellation effect in naive integration as the key challenge, where conflicting representations
487   branches reduce expressivity and hinder convergence. To address this, we introduce the overlapping
488   cancellation ratio to quantify the effect and an alignment loss to encourage cooperative learning.
489   Building on this framework, we instantiate CHTsL by combining the advanced dynamic sparse
490   training method CHTs with low-rank factorization. Extensive experiments on LLaMA-60M and
491   LLaMA-130M with OpenWebText and C4 demonstrate that CHTsL consistently outperforms ex-
492   isting methods under equivalent parameter budgets. Our work is the first to systematically unify
493   dynamic connectivity and spectral sparse training, moving beyond static connectivity sparsity and
494   naive integration; it identifies and mitigates the cancellation effect, fostering effective collaboration
495
      between the sparse and low-rank components; and it provides a practical instantiation that validates
      the benefits of this integration. Overall, this study offers both theoretical insights and practical
496
      solutions for efficient sparse pre-training, highlighting the potential of combining complementary
497
      sparsity paradigms to maximize model expressivity under constrained resources.

      A    S PARSITY C ONFIGURATION FOR LL A MA-60M AND LL A MA-130M
649
650
      The sparsity configuration for methods combining a sparse branch with a low-rank branch is defined
651   by two values: s, the sparsity of the connectivity-sparse component, and r, the rank of the low-rank
652   component.
653
654
      In our experiments, for each fixed total sparsity, we varied the sparsity-configuration in steps of
      ∆s = 0.05. That is, whenever the parameter count of one branch was reduced by 5% relative
655
      to dense training, the parameter count of the other branch was increased accordingly. Since the
656
      sparsity of the connectivity-sparse branch is directly tied to the total sparsity, the main challenge
657   is determining the corresponding rank adjustment in the low-rank branch, which depends on the
658   structure of the LLaMA model.
659
660
      All linear layers in LLaMA are replaced by our sparse components. Because LLaMA models of
      different sizes are built from repeated Transformer blocks with identical architecture, it suffices to
661
      analyze a single block to establish the relationship between s and r. Each block contains seven linear
662
      layers, denoted as Q, K, V, O, up, down, and gate. Among them, Q, K, V, and O have weight matrices
663   of size h × h, while up, down, and gate have size h × f , where h is the embedding dimension and
664   f is the feed-forward dimension. Hence, the step size of the rank rstep corresponding to ∆s = 0.05
665   is determined by:
666
667                               4(h + h)rstep + 3(h + f )rstep
668                                                              = ∆s = 0.05.                            (7)
                                      4(h × h) + 3(h × f )
669
670   For LLaMA-60M with h = 512 and f = 1376, this yields a rank step size of approximately rstep ≈
671   16. For LLaMA-130M with h = 768 and f = 2048, the corresponding step size is rstep ≈ 24.

       F     OCR AND G LOBAL C OSINE S IMILARITY
1027
1028
       To better understand the cancellation phenomenon between the sparse and low-rank branches, we
1029   compare the proposed Overlap Cancellation Ratio (OCR) with commonly used global cosine simi-
1030   larity. While these metrics are related, they capture fundamentally different aspects of cancellation.
1031
1032   F.1   C ONCEPTUAL D IFFERENCE
1033
1034   Global cosine similarity measures only directional alignment between vectors but ignores magni-
1035   tude, which is crucial for assessing the severity of cancellation. In contrast, OCR explicitly quanti-
1036   fies the fraction of overlapping magnitude that is canceled due to opposite signs.
1037   This distinction can be illustrated by the following examples, both with cosine similarity equal to 0:
1038
1039          • Example A (strong cancellation): S = [10, −10], L = [10, 10]. Then S + L = [20, 0],
1040            indicating half cancellation. OCR = 0.5 ((ignoring the small ϵ in the denominator).
1041          • Example B (minimal cancellation): S = [100, 0], L = [0, 100]. Then S +L = [100, 100],
1042            indicating almost no cancellation. OCR = 0.
1043
1044   These examples demonstrate that while cosine similarity may indicate similar or opposite directions,
1045   it does not capture the magnitude of signal lost. OCR complements cosine similarity by explicitly
1046   measuring this magnitude-based cancellation.
1047
1048   F.2   E MPIRICAL O BSERVATION WITH C OSINE S IMILARITY
1049
1050
       To provide a thorough observation, we include here cosine similarity plots corresponding to the
       training of LLaMA-60M on OpenWebText under different total sparsities (0.9, 0.8, 0.7) as a sup-
1051
       plement. The settings are exactly the same as the reported in Section 5.1 and Section C where OCR
1052
       plots are reported. Figures 6, 7, and 8 show the evolution of cosine similarity between the outputs
1053   of the sparse and low-rank branches during training.
1054
1055   These curves demonstrate that the alignment-enhanced training scheme increases directional align-
1056
       ment between the two branches, with a notably higher cosine similarity observed in the Q and K
       layers. This observation complements the OCR measure, confirming that the alignment loss not
1057
       only reduces magnitude-based cancellation but also improves global directional alignment.

       G    F URTHER D ISCUSSION : A PPLYING A LIGNMENT L OSS O NLY TO Q,K
1243
1244
            L AYERS
1245
       According to the OCR plots, when applying the alignment loss to all linear layers, the cancellation
1246
       effect is primarily mitigated in the Q and K layers rather than uniformly across all layers. This
1247
       observation motivates a more efficient approach: applying the alignment loss exclusively to the Q
1248   and K layers.
1249
1250   To investigate this, we conducted experiments with CHTsL applying alignment loss only to Q and K
1251
       layers (Align qk) and only except Q/K layers (Align others), compared with those obtained by ap-
       plying alignment loss to all layers (Align all), using exactly the same best hyperparameter settings
1252
       as summarized in Section B. The results, presented in Table 7, demonstrate that restricting the align-
1253
       ment loss to Q and K layers yields similar or even improved performance compared to applying it to
1254   all layers. Align Q/K layers significantly outperforms align other linear layers, with the Wilcoxon
1255   signed-rank p-value lower than 0.05.
1256
1257
       These findings further validate that OCR accurately captures the cancellation effect and highlight
       that the alignment loss can be implemented more efficiently, achieving comparable or better results
1258
       with reduced computational cost. Moreover, this observation provides a useful perspective for future
1259
       work building upon this study, suggesting that targeted alignment may be sufficient to achieve strong
1260   performance while saving computation.

       In this section, we report the inference memory usage and throughput for CHTsL, SLTrain, and
1461   the dense baseline. For CHTsL and SLTrain, the sparsity configuration was set to dconnectivity :
1462   dspectral = 1 : 1, under a total sparsity 0.9. Each model was run for 5000 inference steps with
1463   dummy inputs of batch size 128 and sequence length 256. We record the maximum memory usage
1464   using torch.cuda.max memory allocated (in GB) and measure the average throughput
1465   (Tokens/sec). Experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA A100-80GB GPU.
1466
       The inference memory and throughput of CHTsL are theoretically identical to those of SLTrain, as
1467   the two differ only in the training procedure while sharing the same inference-stage architecture.
1468   Both methods gain efficiency from:
1469
1470         • sparse matrix multiplication in the connectivity-sparse branch
1471         • low-rank multiplication in the spectral-sparse branch.
1472
1473   For accurate inference benchmarking, we evaluated the trained CHTsL checkpoints using the
1474   SLTrain C++ codebase. For CHTsL, two minimal modifications were made: (1) adding the ac-
1475   tivation function required by the spectral-sparse branch, and (2) correcting the computation order
1476   in the low-rank branch, which in the original SLTrain implementation computed B@A and then
1477
       B@A@X and thus introduced redundant operations.
1478   Table 12 shows that CHTsL achieves both lowest memory usage and highest throughput out of
1479   three models. The advantage in throughput arises from the corrected low-rank computation order,
1480   as mentioned above in the second modification. This demonstrates that CHTsL already provides
1481   practical efficiency benefits while holding even greater theoretical potential.
1482   Finally, we emphasize that neither CHTsL nor SLTrain can fully realize their theoretical speed-ups
1483   due to current software and hardware limitations. PyTorch does not provide efficient kernels for un-
1484   structured sparsity, and modern GPUs offer minimal acceleration for unstructured sparse operations.
1485   Thus, all unstructured sparse methods are currently operating below their theoretical limits.
1486
       Overall, CHTsL outperforms the dense baseline in both memory and speed, and its efficiency ad-
1487   vantage is likely to increase further as frameworks and hardware improve support for unstructured
1488   sparsity.
